A targeted social-engineering attack that used a lookalike single sign-on portal, phone-based employee impersonation, and abuse of MFA push to gain temporary access to a single user identity. The incident, documented by ReliaQuest and detected on August 22, 2026, did not result in access to customer data, business applications, or internal systems, Although one employee entered credentials on a fraudulent page and approved a push notification, layered identity and device security controls limited the attacker’s access and enabled rapid containment. ReliaQuest Thwarts Social Engineering Attack ReliaQuest said claims that it suffered a ransomware compromise or broader breach are false. The operation began with the registration of a domain that resembled a legitimate ReliaQuest property. Threat actors then deployed a counterfeit ReliaQuest SSO page behind a content delivery network, a tactic that can help obscure hosting infrastructure and support fast, disposable phishing campaigns. The attackers called several employees and impersonated members of the company’s security team by name. The calls were intended to direct targets toward the malicious authentication page and persuade them to complete the login process. One employee ultimately submitted a password and accepted an MFA push notification, allowing the attackers to obtain a short-lived session in ReliaQuest’s identity dashboard. The session provided only view-level access. Attackers attempted to use that session to access additional applications through the dashboard, but their attempts were consistently denied. ReliaQuest’s device-trust controls prevented unapproved or non-corporate devices from accessing company applications and systems. This separation between identity authentication and application authorization was central to stopping the intrusion. A valid username, password, and MFA approval did not automatically grant the attackers access to sensitive resources. The company then terminated the malicious session, expired the affected employee’s password, and reset every authentication factor associated with the account. ReliaQuest conducted a review of its security-control fidelity, device-trust enforcement, on-network activity, and suspicious events during the 48 hours following the incident. The investigation found that no additional identities were accessed and that the attackers did not reach business applications, customer environments, or internal infrastructure. No customer or ReliaQuest data was accessed beyond the compromised user’s login credentials, the company said. Investigators also found no evidence of persistence, indicating that the threat actors did not establish a durable foothold through new accounts, authentication changes, malware, remote tools, or other access mechanisms. The campaign reflects an increasingly common enterprise intrusion pattern. Attackers combine convincing social engineering with technical infrastructure designed to steal credentials and exploit weak MFA workflows. A disposable lookalike domain may be registered and abandoned within hours, while a phishing page hosted behind a CDN can make the operation appear more legitimate and complicate response efforts. Voice calls add pressure and credibility, especially when attackers know employee names, organizational roles, or internal terminology. Once a victim accepts an unsolicited MFA request, threat actors may try to enroll a new authenticator, access cloud applications, modify recovery options, or escalate privileges. The incident underscores why organizations should assume that phishing can eventually succeed against an individual user. Security awareness training remains important, but it should not be the final control protecting critical systems. Enterprises should require phishing-resistant authentication where possible, enforce device posture and conditional access policies, restrict identity dashboard privileges, and alert on unusual MFA activity or new authenticator enrollments. Employees should independently verify unexpected calls from security personnel and reject MFA prompts they did not initiate. ReliaQuest’s response demonstrates that strong device trust, segmented authorization, and rapid identity containment can turn a successful phishing event into a limited, unsuccessful intrusion. Give your security team the visibility and context to investigate suspicious activity faster and contain threats before business impact grows.
